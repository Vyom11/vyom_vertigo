So I was sitting at my desk in office, scrolling through Reels to find inspiration for a design I wanted to make. (The real reason, of course, was to complete my 8 hours and go home, but let's keep that between us.) Somewhere between an Instagram guru explaining how waking up at 4 AM would unlock my chakras and an AI cat dancing (which, as a staunch hater of AI, is my guilty pleasure—but hey, we all have our vices), I had a flashback to my previous internship.

There, I was once told that if I only completed the company-mandated 8 hours, then it said a lot about my character. Apparently, leaving on time wasn't just poor work ethic—it was a personality flaw. According to them, I would never be able to succeed, not only in that company but in life itself.

Now here's where the company deserves credit. They understood psychology far better than I did.

If I completed exactly 8 hours, the attendance portal would show... nothing. Just a plain grey entry. But if I stayed for 9 hours, suddenly my attendance turned green, as if I had personally increased India's GDP.

Somewhere, Pavlov was smiling.

They had managed to train an entire office with nothing more than a coloured rectangle.

They weren't rewarding productivity.

They were rewarding visibility.

The funny part is that I wasn't actually working for those 9 hours.

My day usually went something like this: spend two hours asking ChatGPT for a solution, refine it with Gemini because ChatGPT didn't understand my vibe, forward both answers to Claude, and then spend another thirty minutes screaming at Claude for confidently generating code that had absolutely no intention of running. Once the task was meaningfully done and worthy of being shared as a respectable end-of-day update, the remaining six hours were devoted to enjoying free coffee, staring intensely at my screen whenever someone walked by, and strategically opening random GitHub tabs so I looked like I was making the company its next unicorn.

Don't forget that none of this work required me to physically stay in the office. I could have gone home, completed the task using my own network, laptop, and sanity, understood it better, and still delivered it on time.

But that little green checkmark was strangely addictive. It felt like progress, even when nothing productive had happened.

Thankfully, my current workplace is nothing like that. I finish my work, leave at the designated time, and I'm home by 8 eating homemade food instead of reaching close to 11 at night, convinced that I'd earned Pavlov's chocolate.

And that's when a thought struck me.

Maybe the problem was never that one company. Maybe we've become so accustomed to measuring commitment by the number of hours someone is visible rather than the amount of work they actually produce that we don't even question it anymore.This isn't just an office problem.

It's a habit.

We are conditioned to believe that if someone higher up tells us to sit for twelve hours, then sitting for twelve hours must automatically be productive. We confuse obedience with dedication and presence with contribution. The reality is that most of us probably do four hours of meaningful work and spend the rest daydreaming, taking coffee breaks, and pretending to look busy. (To be fair, daydreaming is hard work too, so don't beat yourself up.)

I feel that we have gotten attuned to delegating our responsibilities to someone else. As long as we are not the captain of the ship and are simply the crew, we are glad to be part of the adventure. But ask us to steer the ship, and that's where the troubles begin.

Now, I get it. The person who takes charge is the one who is remembered. After all, there is a reason why all of Odysseus's men died except for the big guy himself. Or how, in the greatest epic of all, the Mahabharata, out of 3.94 million warriors who fought, only twelve remained alive by the end of the holy war. These were people who took matters into their own hands, accepted the consequences of their choices, and defined their own destiny.

Now, is your life meant to be some form of a holy war?

To a certain extent, yes.

You are the master of your destiny. You are the captain of your ship. Sure, you may receive a signal halyard here or there, but it is ultimately you who has to take hold of the mast and decide whether you reach the pearly gates of heaven or the infernal gates of hell.

For centuries, this tendency to surrender responsibility was rewarded. The reason is quite simple. We accepted orders and ideas from our superiors because, deep down, we believed that if we simply followed them closely enough, maybe we could become like them.

But people don't pass down certainty. They pass down performance.

Everyone wears a mask they believe society wants to see, not necessarily who they really are. And it never occurred to me until I started writing this essay.

Why did I think that if I followed the words of someone I barely knew—the same words being followed by dozens of other people—I would somehow end up with the exact same success? The answer is embarrassingly simple.

Because I never questioned whether those words deserved to be followed in the first place. Now I completely understand why we thought this form of self-inflicted slavery disguised as hustle was a good decision. Sometimes the hare chasing a dangling carrot does stumble upon something greater.

Maybe even a chilled glass of cold coffee.

Wait...

Was I talking about the hare or myself?

Never mind.

I must move ahead with my thoughts.

The bigger epidemic I see today is that we haven't stopped outsourcing responsibility.

We've simply changed who we outsource it to.

Earlier it was teachers.

Then professors.

Then managers.

Now it's ChatGPT.

People spend hours asking ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini questions and call that work. Others proudly label themselves artists, creators, or disruptors because they know how to prompt an LLM. Somewhere along the way, using a tool became indistinguishable from thinking.

And that's the part I honestly don't understand.

Anytime we have to make a decision, our first instinct is to open ChatGPT.

Not think.

Not struggle.

Not fail.

Just ask.

AI can help you streamline, simplify, and organise your thoughts, but it cannot replace the responsibility of having those thoughts in the first place.

Sure, feed it trillions of stolen data points and it will generate something useful. But that AI itself is built upon the work of people who actually created.

Someone still had to write the books.

Compose the music.

Paint the paintings.

Shoot the films.

Design the software.

Live the lives.

By the current trajectory, I genuinely worry about where creativity is heading.

A child is born in a white-light operation theatre (because doctors apparently don't vibe with aesthetic lighting). They grow up glued to Cocomelon before they can form complete sentences. They attend a school where success means remembering the answer long enough to write it in an exam before forgetting it the following week. They move on to a mediocre college where every assignment is delegated to the bots of the Matrix while they chase the next Reel, the next like, and the next notification. Eventually they sit through placements, join an underpaid and overworked company, burn out by twenty-eight, and spend the rest of their lives scrolling through Reels while writing blog posts that nobody is going to read.

Maybe that's why that little green rectangle still bothers me.

Not because it made me stay an extra hour.

But because it taught me something much more dangerous. It taught me that looking productive was more important than being productive. And once you learn that lesson, you start looking for someone else to tell you what success looks like.

Your boss.

Your teacher.

Your parents.

Your favourite LinkedIn influencer.

Or now, your favourite chatbot.

Somewhere, Pavlov is still smiling.
