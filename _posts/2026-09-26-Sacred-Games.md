So I finished reading *Sacred Games* a few months back, and it has been one hell of a ride. The simple reason why I picked up this novel was that I hadn’t really explored Indian literature and writers, so I found this Vikram Chandra venture to be a decent starting point.

Turns out, it was a pretty damn good starting point.

This really is one of the best novels I have ever read. Which is why the serialisation in the form of the first Indian Netflix series titled *Sacred Games* was so disappointing. In fact, I feel that the arrival of that series was the death of Indian OTT series as we knew it. This article is here to elaborate on that.

**Note: Spoilers ahead for Sacred Games Novel and Series**

Let's first start with the plot and how these two differ.

Both the novel and the series follow a simple structure. We follow Inspector Sartaj Singh, who figures out or is given the explicit location of known don Ganesh Gaitonde. Before Sartaj is able to apprehend him, Gaitonde shoots himself in the head with nothing but a warning that something is about to come.

Now it's up to Sartaj to figure out what that scheme is, who the powers at play are, and how to make sure that the city is safe.

The difference lies in how these two tackle that story.

The novel is first and foremost a character-centric story. It plays out like a regular procedural drama where the characters take centre stage, be it Sartaj, Gaitonde, Parulkar, Katekar, and so many more. And the biggest thing is that none of these characters are distinctly good. They all have grey shades in them, where you find yourself supporting them on one page, opposing them on the other, while other times you are simply left contemplating their decisions.

The novel works wonderfully because it focuses on a small group of characters but never lets go of their humanity. That is precisely the reason why, even though both stories tackle the impending doom of a nuclear attack, the novel makes you feel the tension in a very personal manner.

Probably the first time I am saying that “show, don't tell” worked better in a novel.

Sure, the novel too consists of graphic visual writing, curses, and other such details, but they come in naturally to the way a person would talk.

On the flip side, the series completely betrays any form of character writing, in turn focusing on making the show as graphically vulgar as possible, for no reason.

I will go over the characters later down the article, but even when focusing on the overall structure, the difference is obvious. The novel makes us focus on a small group of characters, but the series needs to expand as much as possible. There is no story in the series; it's just one set piece after the next where we can put in one curse word or sex scene after the other with no rhyme or reason.

I guess Anurag and Vikramaditya were so ecstatic at being given the free rein of a web series that they let their fetishes seep through into what should have been a good story.

The best way I can showcase the difference is that the novel is understated in nature. It understands how much a single inspector can achieve with no help, whilst the series treats Sartaj as the macho man who can do anything alone.

And that makes it a good time to focus our thoughts on one of the leads: Sartaj Singh.

The Sartaj Singh of the novel is a thing of beauty.

He is a righteous policeman turned bitter by the realities of the world around him. He sees the crime being committed yet understands he does not really like the people he’s working for. He helps out a woman in a pickle, but makes sure to take a bribe from her.

When someone does not cooperate with him, he does not make the person understand the gravity of what he is trying to investigate. He simply threatens the person, which then leads to some doors getting opened. Most of them remain closed, though those doors are opened when he uses his police brain.

Amidst all this chaos, we also see Sartaj as a lonely man who has accepted his loneliness but can't help but dream of a better future with someone. Even after his divorce, he has not let go of the idea of having a family of his own.

There are many a times when people tell him to get over his divorce and take time to find someone new in his life, and the Sartaj of the novel understands that and does find love and genuine companionship, albeit a bit scared.

What I especially loved is that we are never given much detail as to what were the reasons because of which the cracks occurred between Megha and Sartaj. But the characterisation is so strong that we understand what could have been the reason.

And the beauty in the writing is such that it mimics how an inspector like Sartaj would always have the focus on his case, yet the thoughts of something else would linger every time he let his eyes close.

The series Sartaj is a Bollywood version of the same.

He is this beacon of justice who will do no wrong and is grappled by the ghosts of his past, so much so that he is actively in denial of his divorce to Megha and tries to get back with her over and over again. This makes him seem needy and someone who acts like a greedy dog rather than like a person with some dimensionality.

As we reach the perspective of the dog, we must understand the duality of the person that is Ganesh Gaitonde.

Gaitonde of the novel is a pathetic man who does everything he can to become the god that he thinks he is.

What I personally enjoyed is not his characterisation, rather his relationship with others. How he sees a genuine partnership with Paritosh Shah, how a simple call with Guru Ji makes him see things differently, his relationship with his crew, and so much more where we can see the person Gaitonde is and what he could have been had things been a bit different.

The one thing that will make you understand him even more is that throughout the novel, Gaitonde is always seen in control. No matter the troubles, Gaitonde feels deep down that he will come out on top.

The series Gaitonde is nothing alike.

His relationship with Paritosh is not fleshed out. We don't get to see him talk with his crew normally. One example being how, after the reign of Suleiman Isa increases, Gaitonde realises that he must position himself as the Hindu saviour of Gopalmath. Hence, that leads to him executing Chhota Badriya, even though he understands that he has done nothing wrong.

But it's the fact that Gaitonde has to pick a side that makes him take that difficult choice.

All of that nuance is missing in the series, making the Netflix Gaitonde seem like a headless chicken having illicit relations and not having a grip on reality by the time season 2 ends.

It really is a disappointing characterisation of someone who could have been different.

Now, I could say how Parulkar, Katekar, Majid, and many other such characters are completely different, but it would be wasting your time and my mood.

Because the novel makes these feel like genuine people who understand the realities of the India that they live in.

Majid is a person who is shown to lead a happy life and takes a back seat. In the series, he basically acts like the second lead.

Parulkar of the novel is a father figure to Sartaj and has his best interests at hand, and because of that dynamic, the final betrayal by Sartaj hits us like a rock.

On the other hand, the Parulkar of the series is a simple bribe-taking corrupt officer who gets a moral compass in the final episode of season 2.

So you get my point of how this series does not really understand what the point of the novel was.

The biggest culprit is the way the story is framed in the series.

The novel is very clear that it's a character study that just so happens to have elements of the real world into the mix. The series, due to its changes, makes it much more provocative in nature.

The core problem is Guru Ji.

In the novel, he’s just a catalyst who leads Gaitonde to join in on his plan. But it's only after it's too late that Gaitonde realises what he has partaken in and tries to correct that wrong.

The Guru Ji of the series is given so much unnecessary detail that he comes off as a caricature of a person whose only goal is to destroy the world.

This right-wing mentality of showcasing the Hindu texts as a means to convert people is something that is shoehorned into the mix, which makes the commentary less like a reflection and more like a personal bias of the showrunners and writers.

Another such example is that after the death of Katekar, we focus on the family left behind as well.

Even though Katekar’s wife is distraught, we see her stabilise in her world and grapple with the realities while trying to do good in that world.

All of that is missing in the series, where she simply becomes the token sad character.

Though the biggest crime is done to the sons.

I was in love with how the novel showcased the relation of Katekar’s sons, Mohit and Rohit. One tries to correct his ways and becomes the ideal son, goes to classes, earns money, and tries to support the family, while the other is fixated on comics, fighting, and fuelling that rage in some other shape or form.

We don't really see what happened to them later. We just see Sartaj being scared of how one might turn out in the future and see a reality where he might have to be the one to shoot a bullet through their heart.

All of that subtlety and nuance is thrown out the window, where we see Rohit become a part of a right-wing group, which shows where the leanings of the character lie.

And this is the problem at its core.

The novel in and of itself is expansive in nature, and the series format would have made it very easy for them to adapt it without changing the nature of the story.

India could have had its *The Wire*, its *The Sopranos*.

Except what we got was a mix and match of writers who were enamoured by the idea of getting to make a series no-holds-barred. So they add unnecessary details, add details on how Hindus are at the forefront of evils in the world, add random sex scenes, curses, while still retaining the Bollywood sensibilities.

I genuinely believe that because of the way this series was structured and how big of a hit it became, Indian OTT is still unable to create something truly unique and creative in a way that will stand toe to toe with Western juggernauts.

The DNA of *Sacred Games* still lives on in every other series that India has ever made.

And it's a shame that I can't blow a nuclear bomb onto this series for the Indian OTT revolution to start fresh.
