I am a hack and a fraud. At least, that is what I felt about myself.

Having watched close to 900 films, when I looked at my Letterboxd to gloat about this fact, I was left a bit embarrassed. This was due to the fact that my entire watch consisted of foreign-language cinema. The Scorseses, the Nolans, the Cruises. Whilst Indian cinema mostly consisted of slop and what one would call commercial cinema.

This led to a change in my worldview, where I started my YouTube channel, “The Classics”, revisiting Indian classics such as *Do Bigha Zamin*, *Pyaasa*, *Guide*, and many more. One such name that I chose was *Satya*.

Now, *Satya* was a different film for me because it came up on my radar after I had a question in my mind. Watching the trailers for *Mumbai Saga*, *Mirzapur: The Movie*, and other films like *Maalik*, I was left wondering: are there any films that explore the human side of these gangsters? Films that acknowledge the flaws and cruelty they commit but still show their side of a faulty humanity?

This question got answered for me by two people. The first being Vikram Chandra, and the other being none other than Ram Gopal Varma.

First things first, I am an avid reader, having read more than 150 novels (a not-so-subtle flex, I know), but one that I am currently reading is Vikram Chandra’s *Sacred Games*. I did not watch the series because I knew I wanted to read the novel first, and I was enamoured by the character writing and how human everyone felt.

Though currently I am on page 400-something out of the 900, rest assured, a think piece will come out from my end when the novel gets finished. For today, we focus on *Satya*.

And the first thing that struck me about *Satya* is how strange its protagonist is.

*Satya* as a story feels less concerned with the character of Satya and more with the world that he inhabits. Satya rarely gets any dialogue, yet I feel I understand most of his struggles through his eyes alone.

That is where the brilliance of the character lies.

Satya acts like an audience surrogate. He too is thrust into this distant world along with us, where both of us try to figure out the workings of the underworld. We don't know these people, we don't understand their rules, and we don't know what the hell is going on half the time.

But Satya doesn't either.

And that makes him us.

We feel the same pain, the same joy, and the same wrath. Do I agree with him? Absolutely not. Do I understand him? Kind of. I am just as aware of Satya as a person as Vidya understood him.

And perhaps that is the most important thing about *Satya*. The film never asks you to forgive these people. It simply asks you to understand that they are people.

That distinction is what separates *Satya* from the usual gangster fantasy.

That brings me to the other coterie consisting of Manoj Bajpayee’s Bhiku Mhatre, Saurabh Shukla’s Kallu Mama, Paresh Rawal’s Commissioner Amod Shukla, and many more.

*Satya* is mostly remembered for the iconic performance of Manoj Bajpayee as Bhiku Mhatre and how he left an imprint on the gangster persona. And rightfully so. Bhiku Mhatre is one of those performances where the actor becomes inseparable from the character.

But I was left speechless by two other characters that get little to no recognition.

First is Aditya Srivastava as Inspector Khandilkar.

He plays the role of an inspector with such finesse that I am speechless even to this day. Though, to be fair, Abhijeet was my favourite from *CID*, so I am a bit biased.

But still, he creates a three-dimensional character that could have easily become caricaturish at the hands of a lesser actor.

Instead, you understand where his ideals come from, why he has such a tough exterior, and how he considers Commissioner Amod Shukla his surrogate father. There is a certain instance with him that makes us relate with him even more.

And this is where the writing helps immensely. Khandilkar doesn't feel like “the cop” in a gangster movie. He feels like a person who happens to be a cop.

Honestly, I was left spellbound by Aditya Srivastava the most, except for one other person.

Saurabh Shukla as Kallu Mama feels like a sheep in wolf’s clothing.

I could almost see the human side of him take over the gangster in him. I am avoiding spoilers here, but there’s a scene in a car between Satya and Kallu Mama that is probably one of my favourite scenes of the film.

And it is only because of the emotions that Saurabh Shukla emits during that moment.

Never in a million years did I think that the righteous judge from *Jolly LLB* could play such a layered gangster. I guess that really speaks volumes about the capabilities of Saurabh Shukla as a writer, director, and actor.

There are other powerhouse performances by brilliant actors like Shefali Shah, Makrand Deshpande, Govind Namdev, Sanjay Mishra, and many more, and their contributions make the film stand out even more.

Having no idea about the cast except for Manoj Bajpayee made me do the Leo pointing meme anytime these actors came up on screen, which was just such a joy.

But what makes all of these performances work is that *Satya* doesn't treat its underworld as a collection of larger-than-life movie characters. These are people with egos, insecurities, loyalties, fears, and relationships.

They just happen to also be criminals.

And that is where Ram Gopal Varma's direction becomes so important.

There is a scene in the opening that shows a hit gone wrong, and we see a character running. What I found the most fascinating is that the shot is taken from a high angle where, along with the character, we see the dirty road filled with mud that has accumulated due to the rain.

It is such a small thing, but it adds so much to the atmosphere.

The world feels dirty.

Not “gritty” in the artificial, cinematic way that the word is thrown around today, but genuinely dirty. The characters don't exist in some glamorous underworld where everyone has perfect suits and dramatic lighting. They exist in cramped rooms, dirty streets, cars, police stations, and places that feel lived in.

There is another small detail that has stuck with me. In the movie, there is a betrayal, and in the background we see a Mahabharata painting.

It is such a small detail, but it adds so much to make the movie thematically rich and layered. The people in this film aren't simply good or evil. They have their loyalties, betrayals, friendships, ambitions and moral codes, even if those moral codes are completely fucked.

That is where the film's world starts to feel almost mythological while remaining completely grounded.

And honestly, I could go on and on about the inventive direction and interesting cinematography, even though the songs are terrible (even though this film had Vishal Bhardwaj and Gulzar at the helm of affairs).

I can confidently say that it’s only because of the interesting cinematography and inventive direction that I could stay away from the +10 skip button anytime a new song popped up.

The writing is great, as per usual. I wish I could praise more, but everything that could have been said about the stellar writing by Anurag Kashyap and Saurabh Shukla has already been said at length, and it shows in their body of work as well, so we will leave it at that.

And honestly, I would implore everyone to go in as blind as possible.

Because *Satya* is one of those films where knowing too much about what happens can take away from the experience. Going in without any preconceived notions is probably the best way to watch this film.

And finally, we reach the thing that brought all of this together.

*Satya* makes you confront the realities of the Indian gangster and the underworld. We look at them and treat them as unbridled monsters, which, in many ways, they are. But the film refuses to let us stop there.

Because they are also flesh and blood.

They were born somewhere. They had families. They fell in love. They laughed. They had friends. They had ambitions. They made choices. And eventually, those choices led them here.

That doesn't absolve them.

And I think that is the truth *Satya* hides in plain sight.

Humanising a monster does not mean making the monster innocent.

It means acknowledging that monsters don't arrive in the world fully formed.

They are still human beings, born from the same land, living in the same world as us. And that uncomfortable connection between the person and the monster is something that Ram Gopal Varma and his team are able to tap into in a way very few movies have been able to achieve.

I started this journey with *The Classics* because I was embarrassed that after watching nearly 900 films, I had somehow convinced myself that Indian cinema had little to offer beyond what I considered “commercial cinema”.

*Satya* is one of those films that makes that worldview feel rather embarrassing.

I hope this acts as a great medium for people to tap into what Indian cinema is capable of achieving and look into how India has cinema beyond Satyajit Ray that deserves to be talked about in the same breadth as the world.
